# Jobs a brain can learn

Read this when somebody asks for a job that does not exist yet, or when they
describe a routine they keep doing by hand. It is not a menu to offer them and
it is not a list to install from. It is what a working brain looked like after
a year of being asked for things: the jobs that turned out to be worth having,
and the details that made each one work rather than technically exist.

Nothing here is written yet. Every one of these is a folder you would create at
`.claude/skills/<name>/` in the shape the main instructions describe, when and
only when the person actually asks for that job.

---

## The ten that earned their place

**The day note.** Open or create today's note, then fill it by asking. In the
morning that means what they want out of the day, with any live deadline named
first. In the evening it means what actually happened, what slipped, and what
carries. These are two different jobs wearing one name, and the skill has to
read the clock and pick. The failure is asking evening questions at 8am.

**The week.** Read the week's day notes and write the review they would write
if they could remember Monday on a Sunday. Only what the notes actually say.
Days they did not write are reported as days they did not write, never quietly
filled in. Ends by settling two or three things for next week, agreed out loud
rather than assigned.

**Capture.** A link, a thought, a half sentence. It goes into one inbox folder
with front matter and at least one link to something that already exists, and
it takes one line of theirs to file. If capture costs a conversation, they stop
capturing. Sorting the inbox is a different job and belongs to the week.

**Due dates.** One file, every deadline, sorted by date, nothing else living in
it. The temptation is a folder with a note per assignment; resist it, because
the value is the single scan. Log what it is, what it belongs to, when it is
due, and how much it counts for. That last field is what lets a later job say
whether something is worth the evening.

**Preparing for a test.** Work backwards from the date. Break the material into
sessions, put them on real days with the days off left off, and front-load the
weak material rather than spreading everything evenly. Read what went wrong
before the last test in the same subject and say it out loud. A plan that
ignores the last plan's failure is decoration.

**Studying.** Active recall, not rereading. Ask, wait, mark what they could not
answer, and write those down so the next session opens with them. The written
misses are the whole product; a study note that only records what they covered
is a log, not a loop.

**Notes from a class or a meeting.** Land them in the right folder on the way
in, not in a pile to be sorted later. Link them to whatever they belong to.
Flag anything said to be testable, due, or decided, because that is the part
they will come looking for and it is the part free-form notes bury.

**Saving the session.** Sweep the conversation for what is durable and put each
piece where it belongs: a decision into the notes, a fact about them into their
profile with their approval, something that actually happened between the two
of you into the ledger, and a plain log line into the day. Then stop. This is
the job that keeps the assistant from waking up empty every morning, and it is
the one most likely to be skipped, because nothing external ever demands it.

**Tidying the whole folder.** Read everything, ask a short round of questions
about how they actually work, then propose a tiered plan and move nothing until
they say yes. Never delete. Archive instead, and rewrite the links that break.
This one is slow and rare and worth having anyway, because a knowledge folder
decays quietly and nobody notices from inside it.

**Talking through a real decision.** Several independent readers, each with one
angle, each reading the actual files, arguing before anything is committed. It
earns its keep only on decisions with a cost attached. On a small question it is
theatre, and doing it anyway teaches them to stop asking.

## What the ten have in common

**The description is the trigger, and lazy words are the real ones.** People
type "whats due", not "please display my assignment tracker". A description
written in tidy language never fires.

**One file beats many for anything scanned; one folder per thing beats one file
for anything read.** Deadlines, grades, a reading list: one file. Notes, essays,
projects: a folder each.

**Say where it gets written, exactly.** A vague destination produces files
nobody finds twice. Real folder, real filename pattern, in the instructions.

**Never invent a date, a number, or a fact.** Unknown means ask or mark it
unknown. Writing this rule into the file holds far better than remembering it,
and this is the failure that destroys trust fastest, because a made-up deadline
looks exactly like a real one.

**Check the record before advising.** Anything that gives an opinion on how they
are doing reads the tracker first. Advice from memory is guessing with
confidence.

**Living files carry a last-updated; events carry a date.** Mixing the two makes
the folder impossible to sort later.

**Anything that deletes, overwrites, or sends confirms first.** Every time,
including the second time in one conversation.

**Plain instructions before scripts.** Most of these ten are a page of prose and
nothing else. Reach for code only when instructions genuinely cannot do the job.

## Jobs that need something outside the folder

Reading their mail, their messages, their calendar, their music, a site they are
logged into. All of these are real and all of them share one shape: a small
read-only script that hands back plain text, kept in `scripts/`, with the
instructions doing the thinking. The rules are not negotiable.

- **No credential ever enters the brain folder.** Not a password, not a cookie,
  not a token. If the job cannot work without storing one, the job does not get
  built, and say so plainly rather than building it anyway.
- **Read-only until they explicitly ask otherwise**, and anything that sends
  shows the full text and waits for a yes on that specific send.
- **Whatever the bridge pulls out, gitignore it.** Contacts, message text, mail,
  location history. The instructions can live in the open; the output cannot.
- **Say what it touched.** A job that reads their private data reports that it
  read it, in a line, every time.

These are the jobs most worth having and the ones most worth being slow about.

---

*Ten jobs took a year to arrive. Building all ten on day one is how you end up with ten jobs nobody runs.*
